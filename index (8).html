About Computational Thinking

Solving problems step by step, with or without a computer.

What is computational thinking?

Computational thinking is a fundamental analytical thinking skill set for everyone, regardless of whether it is executed by a human or a machine. The thinking process is split into six steps: decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, algorithm, logical reasoning and evaluation.

Decomposition

Breaking down big problems into several smaller problems that can be managed easily.

Pattern recognition

Identifying recurring properties or similarities across instances.

Abstraction

Focusing solely on the important parts of a problem while ignoring what does not matter.

Algorithm

A precise step-by-step procedure for solving problems or completing tasks.

Logical reasoning

A clear and structured way of thinking that decides whether statements are true, whether inferences are valid and whether the conclusion works.

Evaluation

Determining whether a solution is good enough and fits its purpose.

Mathematics Topic

Year 5 · Coordinates

Coordinate

A coordinate is a number, or a set of numbers, that represents the exact position of a point on a grid, on a map or in space. In daily life, coordinates are commonly used in GPS, computer graphics, game development and mathematical graphing.

In mathematics, coordinates are used to graph functions and equations. They are also used in geometric transformations, calculus, mathematical analysis and vector mathematics.

Unplugged Activity

Minimal or no digital technology.

What are unplugged activities?

Unplugged activities are low-cost, hands-on learning activities that teach computational thinking to students with little or no use of computers or digital devices. Puzzles, riddles, games, cards, paper and everyday items are often used to carry out the activities.

Unplugged activities play a crucial role in helping students connect their computational thinking skills to real-life situations. This is possible because unplugged activities use familiar physical situations, such as giving directions, sorting objects, planning routes and organising tasks, while applying computational thinking.

Advantages

  • Cheap: they open up opportunities for students in rural areas to develop and practise their computational thinking without any access to computers or digital devices.
  • Social: they require students to work together to solve a puzzle or riddle. They also allow students to engage physically in the activities, making them interactive and engaging.

Disadvantages

It can be difficult to connect the skills learnt in unplugged activities to plugged-in activities.

Dino’s Coordinate Path: The Treasure Quest

Objective: Pupils guide a cut-out paper dinosaur, starting from the origin (0, 0), across a printed Cartesian grid filled with brick walls and water hazards to reach the final treasure chest safely, using the shortest route.

Materials needed

  • Printed study aid: the Dino’s Coordinate Path worksheet grid
  • Cut-out paper dinosaur (used as the movable game piece)
  • A pair of scissors
  • A pencil and an eraser

How to play

  1. Prepare your game piece. Print out the study aid provided. Carefully cut out the dinosaur character; it will serve as your moving game piece on the grid.
  2. Set up the challenge. Teachers can rearrange the challenges and obstacles based on their creativity, creating stages that get progressively harder as pupils advance.
  3. Locate the starting point. Place your cut-out dinosaur at the origin (0, 0), where the x-axis (horizontal) and the y-axis (vertical) intersect.
  4. Plan your route (algorithm design). Look ahead at the grid layout and plan your coordinate sequence before moving. Rule reminder: always move x across first, then y up! For example, (4, 2) means 4 units across, then 2 units up.
  5. Follow the game rules and constraints.
    • No water crossing: the dinosaur cannot cross or step on water hazards.
    • No brick wall collisions: the dinosaur cannot walk through brick walls.
    • Shortest route objective: pupils must work out and follow the shortest possible route to reach the treasure chest.
  6. Debug and correct errors. If your path forces you to cross water or hit a brick wall, your coordinate sequence contains an error (often caused by swapping x and y). Debug your path: recalculate your coordinates, adjust your route and guide your dinosaur safely to the treasure!

Plugged-in Activity

Learning with digital tools.

What are plugged-in activities?

Plugged-in activities are problem-solving activities that use computers, tablets, robots, coding apps or other digital tools to help learners develop computational thinking skills. Plugged-in activities are often a continuation of an unplugged activity. For example, students might first learn sequencing by arranging paper command cards (unplugged), then program the same sequence in Scratch (plugged-in). The plugged-in version lets them immediately test, run and debug their instructions.

Advantages

  • Direct practice with real technology
  • Immediate feedback and debugging
  • Higher engagement and motivation
  • Creative production
  • Authentic digital skills

Disadvantages

Students in rural areas might not have access to any computers or digital devices, and it is even worse when there is no internet connection in their area. This might cause some students with hidden talent not to be discovered early, which could stunt their growth potential.

What is Kurier Harapan?

Kurier Harapan (The Kind Delivery Courier) is an interactive, web-based digital game designed specifically for Year 5 Mathematics (topic: Coordinates). In this game, pupils take on the role of a kind delivery courier whose mission is to navigate a Cartesian coordinate grid to deliver packages to specific addresses or recipients across the town.

Instead of traditional, dry worksheet drills, pupils use digital coordinate inputs (x, y) to guide the courier’s vehicle from the starting depot to the correct destination on the grid.

How Kurier Harapan helps pupils learn computational thinking (CT)

The game seamlessly embeds the six core pillars of computational thinking into gameplay:

  1. Decomposition (breaking down complex routes). Delivering a package across a large grid can be overwhelming. Pupils break the main delivery mission into smaller, sequential steps: identifying the starting location, determining intermediate checkpoints and pinpointing the final destination (x, y).
  2. Pattern recognition (observing grid regularities). As pupils move the courier across multiple rounds, they begin to notice numerical patterns, such as how increasing the x-value moves the courier right across the columns and increasing the y-value moves it up the rows. They recognise predictable coordinate rules for horizontal and vertical travel.
  3. Abstraction (focusing on essential mathematical data). While the game features engaging delivery visuals and graphics, pupils learn to filter out non-essential elements and focus strictly on the core mathematical variables: the ordered pairs (x, y), the grid boundaries and the directional axes.
  4. Algorithm design (creating step-by-step delivery plans). To complete a delivery successfully, pupils must plan and input a logical, precise sequence of coordinate commands. This sequential instruction-giving mirrors coding an algorithm to direct movement on a coordinate plane.
  5. Evaluation and debugging (instant error correction). If a pupil accidentally enters the wrong axis order (for example, swapping x and y) or crashes into a restricted zone, the digital system provides immediate feedback by blocking the move. The pupil must inspect their input, identify the mistake and debug their coordinate values to succeed.
  6. Generalisation (applying rules to new situations). By mastering delivery routes in Kurier Harapan, pupils internalise the universal mathematical rule “read x across, then y up”. They can then transfer this mental model to solve standard exam questions, paper graphs and real-world map-reading tasks.

CT Analysis

How the CT elements appear in the activities.

1. Decomposition

  • Unplugged activity (Dino paper grid): Students break down the task of navigating from the origin (0, 0) to the final treasure chest into smaller, manageable movement chunks (for example, clearing the lower brick wall, routing around the water hazard, collecting the coin and bypassing the upper wall).
  • Plugged-in activity (digital game): The game breaks the coordinate navigation puzzle down into discrete levels, requiring pupils to analyse and input specific (x, y) coordinate pairs step by step to progress.

2. Pattern recognition

  • Unplugged activity (Dino paper grid): Pupils observe spatial patterns on the Cartesian plane, noticing that points sharing the same horizontal row have identical y-values while x increases, and that vertical columns share the same x-values while y changes.
  • Plugged-in activity (digital game): Learners recognise recurring numerical sequences as the character moves across the digital grid, identifying predictable patterns in coordinate placement and boundary rules.

3. Abstraction

  • Unplugged activity (Dino paper grid): Students filter out unnecessary distractions (such as the dinosaur’s pixel-art style or the background grid visuals) and focus strictly on the essential mathematical elements: ordered pairs (x, y), directional steps and coordinate boundaries.
  • Plugged-in activity (digital game): The digital interface abstracts real-world movement into a clean 2D Cartesian plane, allowing students to focus purely on numerical positioning without physical clutter.

4. Algorithm design

  • Unplugged activity (Dino paper grid): Pupils design a precise, sequential list of directional steps or coordinate instructions (moving right along the x-axis, then up along the y-axis) to guide the dinosaur successfully without colliding with obstacles.
  • Plugged-in activity (digital game): Learners construct logical sequences of coordinate inputs, writing a set of rules that the software processes to navigate the character through the digital maze.

5. Evaluation and debugging

  • Unplugged activity (Dino paper grid): When a student’s manually drawn path crosses into a brick wall or water hazard, it reveals an axis-swapping error. The student inspects their written steps, erases the mistake and debugs their path to find a safe route.
  • Plugged-in activity (digital game): The digital tool provides instant feedback. If an entered coordinate lands on an illegal obstacle, the game rejects or blocks the move, prompting pupils to re-evaluate their logic and fix their coordinate inputs in real time.

6. Generalisation

  • Unplugged activity (Dino paper grid): Students internalise the core universal mathematical rule: “read x across, then y up” (x first, then y). This mental model can then be applied to any grid size, map layout or graphing problem.
  • Plugged-in activity (digital game): Learners transfer the coordinate rules and spatial reasoning skills mastered in the digital game to solve broader mathematical problems across different graphing topics.

Comparison

Unplugged and plugged-in, side by side.

Reflection

What I learned from building this website.

First of all, I want to give glory to my Lord for allowing me to do this continuous assessment. Without all the strength and knowledge He provided, I would not have been able to finish this website. I want to thank my lecturer, Dr. Ng Lee Fong, for guiding me throughout the process of making this website. As a teacher trainee, I will be honest that this is my first time creating a website that will be used as a teaching aid. I want to apologise in advance for any flaws and imperfections that my website might have.

While working on this project, building both the unplugged dinosaur grid activity and the digital game Kurier Harapan really opened my eyes as a trainee teacher. Honestly, when we talk about coordinates in Year 5 Mathematics, it sounds simple on paper: just go across for x and up for y. But when you actually think about how kids learn, students get confused all the time, especially by mixing up the order of the axes or miscalculating their paths.

What I learned from combining both unplugged and plugged-in approaches is how well they balance each other out. The paper-based dinosaur grid gives kids a tangible, hands-on feel where they can physically trace with a pencil and visualise the grid lines right in front of them. Meanwhile, Kurier Harapan adds that digital excitement and engagement. The best part about the digital game is the instant feedback: if a student swaps their x and y values or hits a wall, the game blocks them right away. They don’t have to wait for me to check their paper; they can spot their error immediately and figure out how to fix it.

Bringing computational thinking (CT) into this project also changed how I view mathematics teaching. Before this, I used to think CT was strictly for computer science or coding. But breaking down a delivery route into steps (decomposition), noticing how coordinate rows and columns repeat (pattern recognition) and correcting a wrong input (debugging) are actually everyday problem-solving skills in mathematics.

Overall, putting this website together taught me that teaching mathematics isn’t just about formulas or rote memorisation. It’s about creating fun, interactive experiences where kids can experiment, make mistakes and learn by doing. I’m really excited to bring this hands-on mindset into my future classroom.
