About Computational Thinking

Solving problems step by step, with or without a computer.

What is computational thinking?

Computational thinking is a fundamental analytical thinking skill set for everyone, regardless of whether it is executed by a human or a machine. The thinking process is split into six steps: decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, algorithm, logical reasoning and evaluation.

Decomposition

Breaking down big problems into several smaller problems that can be managed easily.

Pattern recognition

Identifying recurring properties or similarities across instances.

Abstraction

Focusing solely on the important parts of a problem while ignoring what does not matter.

Algorithm

A precise step-by-step procedure for solving problems or completing tasks.

Logical reasoning

A clear and structured way of thinking that decides whether statements are true, whether inferences are valid and whether the conclusion works.

Evaluation

Determining whether a solution is good enough and fits its purpose.

Mathematics Topic

Year 5 · Coordinates

Coordinate

A coordinate is a number, or a set of numbers, that represents the exact position of a point on a grid, on a map or in space. In daily life, coordinates are commonly used in GPS, computer graphics, game development and mathematical graphing.

In mathematics, coordinates are used to graph functions and equations. They are also used in geometric transformations, calculus, mathematical analysis and vector mathematics.

Unplugged Activity

Minimal or no digital technology.

What are unplugged activities?

Unplugged activities are low-cost, hands-on learning activities that teach computational thinking to students with little or no use of computers or digital devices. Puzzles, riddles, games, cards, paper and everyday items are often used to carry out the activities.

Unplugged activities play a crucial role in helping students connect their computational thinking skills to real-life situations. This is possible because unplugged activities use familiar physical situations, such as giving directions, sorting objects, planning routes and organising tasks, while applying computational thinking.

Advantages

  • Cheap: they open up opportunities for students in rural areas to develop and practise their computational thinking without any access to computers or digital devices.
  • Social: they require students to work together to solve a puzzle or riddle. They also allow students to engage physically in the activities, making them interactive and engaging.

Disadvantages

It can be difficult to connect the skills learnt in unplugged activities to plugged-in activities.

Plugged-in Activity

The digital tool must have a pedagogical purpose.

What are plugged-in activities?

Plugged-in activities are problem-solving activities that use computers, tablets, robots, coding apps or other digital tools to help learners develop computational thinking skills. Plugged-in activities are often a continuation of an unplugged activity. For example, students might first learn sequencing by arranging paper command cards (unplugged), then program the same sequence in Scratch (plugged-in). The plugged-in version lets them immediately test, run and debug their instructions.

Advantages

  • Direct practice with real technology
  • Immediate feedback and debugging
  • Higher engagement and motivation
  • Creative production
  • Authentic digital skills

Disadvantages

Students in rural areas might not have access to any computers or digital devices, and it is even worse when there is no internet connection in their area. This might cause some students with hidden talent not to be discovered early, which could stunt their growth potential.
